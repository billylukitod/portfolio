## Project Overview
This project aimed to replace standard RC remote controllers with a highly intuitive, gesture-based wearable interface to control a semi-autonomous drone. By mapping natural hand kinematics to standard flight dynamics (pitch, roll, yaw, and throttle), we sought to lower the learning curve for novice pilots and allow for multi-tasking during flight operations.

## Context and Problem
Standard two-stick RC transmitters require significant muscle memory and cognitive load, creating a barrier to entry for operators in search-and-rescue or rapid-deployment scenarios. Existing gesture controllers often suffered from high latency, making agile flight impossible, or relied on vision-based systems which are heavily constrained by lighting and line-of-sight. We needed a low-latency, RF-based wearable solution.

## My Role
I acted as the Team Co-Leader and Head of the Electrical Division. My responsibilities centered on designing the hardware ecosystem and writing the embedded firmware. I architected the ESP32-based wearable, wrote the sensor-fusion and communication algorithms in C/C++, and mentored junior members on PCB design and FreeRTOS task scheduling.

## Objectives
- Design and fabricate a compact, lightweight wearable PCB hosting an ESP32 and an IMU.
- Develop a sensor fusion algorithm to accurately capture complex hand gestures in 3D space without gimbal lock.
- Establish a low-latency wireless link capable of transmitting control matrices to the drone's flight controller.
- Map distinct gestures to complex macro-commands (e.g., auto-takeoff, return-to-home).
- Keep system latency strictly below 50ms to ensure real-time responsiveness.

## System Architecture
The ecosystem is split into two nodes: the Wearable Transmitter and the Drone Receiver. 
- **Wearable Node:** An ESP32 interfaces with an MPU6050 IMU via I2C. The ESP32 processes the raw accelerometer and gyroscope data, computes the Euler angles, and detects discrete gesture events.
- **Communication Link:** Data is serialized and transmitted over a custom low-overhead UDP protocol via a localized Wi-Fi mesh, or optionally via an ExpressLRS (ELRS) module.
- **Drone Node:** An onboard receiver interprets the UDP/RF packets, translates them into the Crossfire (CRSF) protocol, and feeds them into a standard Betaflight/INAV flight controller to actuate the drone's ESCs and motors.

## Technical Approach
To achieve deterministic timing and prevent communication blocking from interrupting sensor reads, I utilized FreeRTOS on the ESP32. I assigned IMU polling to a high-priority hardware timer task, while UDP transmission operated on a separate core. 
For the orientation math, I implemented a Mahony filter to fuse the gyro and accel data, mitigating drift and avoiding the heavy computational cost of a full Kalman filter.

## Engineering Challenges
The most aggressive challenge was managing latency. Initially, standard TCP/IP networking introduced variable delays of up to 120ms, rendering the drone unflyable. I had to strip down the network stack to use bare UDP and optimize the packet structure to a minimal byte array.
Another major issue was IMU drift over time. Aggressive hand movements caused the reference frame to skew. I implemented a robust auto-zeroing routine that dynamically re-calibrated the center point when the user held their hand still for 2 seconds.

## Decisions and Trade-offs
- **FreeRTOS over Bare Metal:** We accepted the slight overhead of an RTOS in exchange for rock-solid task scheduling, which was critical when handling concurrent I2C reads and RF transmission.
- **UDP over Bluetooth (BLE):** We chose UDP via a localized Wi-Fi mesh over BLE because UDP provided superior range and we could control the packet delivery timeline more aggressively.
- **Mahony Filter vs Kalman Filter:** Selected the Mahony filter for sensor fusion to save CPU cycles, allowing a much faster IMU polling rate.

## Limitations
The primary limitation is battery life on the wearable due to the power-hungry Wi-Fi radio on the ESP32, lasting only about 45 minutes of continuous use. Additionally, extreme temperature changes can still introduce slight thermal drift in the IMU, requiring occasional manual recalibration during long operational windows.

## Lessons Learned
This project highlighted the immense importance of deterministic timing in embedded systems. I learned how to effectively profile FreeRTOS tasks and how a seemingly minor block in a communication loop can critically destabilize a real-time physical system. Moving forward, I am investigating custom RF protocols (like nRF24 or LoRa) to replace Wi-Fi for even lower latency and higher energy efficiency.
